Study Abroad on a Budget: 8 Affordable European Countries Under $800/Month in 2026

By Amara DialloPublished June 16, 2026⏳ 12 min read

Most study abroad guides aimed at budget-conscious international students are written for people with comfortable savings. They discuss European cities without mentioning that a student budget under 800 dollars per month is not just possible in parts of Europe: it is the norm for domestic students in several countries, and international students who do their research can live within it too. The expensive version of studying in Europe is real. So is a dramatically more affordable version that most guides never cover because it requires knowing where to look.

Poland: Warsaw and Krakow Under $700/Month

Poland has become one of the most popular study destinations in Europe for international students, and the cost of living explains most of it. Warsaw and Krakow both offer student budgets of €600–€750/month including rent, food, transport, and occasional entertainment. University of Warsaw and Jagiellonian University in Krakow both have strong English-taught programs at the Masters level, with tuition fees for international students of €1,000–€3,000/year - dramatically lower than Western European equivalents. Student dormitory housing in Krakow costs €100–€200/month. It's hard to find a comparable deal in any comparable university city in Western Europe. Search English-taught programs at the Polish government's education portal.

Czech Republic: Prague as a Budget Capital

Prague remains cheaper than its fame and central location suggest. A student budget of €700–€900/month is realistic in Prague, covering a decent room in a shared apartment (€350–€500), food (€150–€200), and transit (€15/month for students). Charles University's English-taught programs cost €3,000–€6,000/year for international students. Czech Technical University is particularly strong for engineering and computer science. The Czech government also funds scholarships for Czech-language programs at no tuition for qualified international students. Study in Czech Republic.

Hungary: Budapest for €600–€800/Month

Budapest is one of the most underrated cities in Europe for quality of life relative to cost. Student accommodation runs €200–€350/month, food is affordable (a university canteen meal costs €2–€4), and the Semesterticket equivalent gives unlimited transit. Corvinus University of Budapest and Budapest University of Technology both have solid international programs. Stipendium Hungaricum covers full tuition and free housing for scholarship holders. For self-funded students, total costs including €2,000–€3,000/year tuition can come in under €10,000/year total. studyinhungary.hu.

Romania: Bucharest and Cluj Under $600/Month

Romania is the cheapest EU member state for study costs and living expenses in 2026. Bucharest student budget: €450–€650/month. Cluj-Napoca is slightly more affordable. Romania's government scholarship program funds 100 spots for non-EU students annually at zero tuition in Romanian-taught programs. English-taught programs at Romanian universities cost €1,500–€3,000/year. Babes-Bolyai University in Cluj is particularly well-regarded for social sciences and humanities. Romanian government scholarship details.

Bulgaria: Sofia for €400–€600/Month

Bulgaria offers the lowest cost of living in the EU. Sofia student budget: €400–€550/month. Sofia University "St. Kliment Ohridski" has English-taught programs, and several private Bulgarian universities offer affordable programs with EU-recognized degrees. The Bulgarian government scholarship for non-EU students covers tuition in Bulgarian-taught programs. For budget-conscious students willing to learn Bulgarian, this is the most affordable fully EU-accredited degree path in Europe.

Portugal: Porto and Coimbra Under $800/Month

Lisbon has become expensive over the last five years, but Porto and Coimbra remain affordable. Porto student budget: €700–€900/month. University of Porto and University of Coimbra (one of Europe's oldest universities, founded 1290) both have international programs in English. Portugal is particularly popular for students who want EU education quality with post-study potential in a country that's become a tech hub - Lisbon's tech scene has grown dramatically. Tuition at Portuguese public universities for non-EU students: approximately €3,000–€4,000/year. Study in Portugal portal.

Spain: Valencia and Salamanca Under $800/Month

Madrid and Barcelona are not budget-friendly. Valencia, Salamanca, and Granada are. Valencia has become particularly popular - good weather, affordable housing (€350–€500/month for a room), strong universities, and a growing English-speaking expat community. University of Valencia tuition for non-EU students: €1,300–€3,500/year. If you speak Spanish or are willing to learn, the value is exceptional. Complutense University in Madrid has programs that cost under €2,500/year in tuition despite being one of Spain's top institutions.

Estonia: Tallinn for €700–€900/Month

Estonia is the most digitally advanced country in the EU and the most overlooked for international study. Tallinn student budget: €700–€900/month. The University of Tartu and Tallinn University of Technology both offer English-taught programs at relatively low tuition for non-EU students (€2,000–€5,000/year). Estonia's residency programs and digital nomad visa make it an interesting choice for tech-focused students who want EU credentials and the option to stay and work afterward.

Building a Budget That Survives Contact With Reality

Most students budget by adding up rent, food, and transport, then discover in month three that the real figure is thirty percent higher. The gap is always in the same places.

Build the number in four layers rather than one. Fixed monthly covers rent, utilities, insurance, phone, and transport, predictable and easy to research. Variable monthly covers food, household goods, and social life, and is the layer students underestimate most consistently. Annual or irregular covers the semester contribution, residence permit renewal, textbooks, winter clothing, and a flight home. One-off setup covers the deposit, furniture, kitchen equipment, and the first month before any income exists.

That fourth layer is the one that causes trouble. Arriving in Warsaw or Prague with exactly one month's rent plus a deposit leaves nothing for a bed, a duvet, plates, or a SIM card, and these come to several hundred euros in the first fortnight.

A workable rule for Central and Eastern Europe: take your researched monthly figure, add fifteen percent for the things you have not thought of, and separately hold two months of that figure as a buffer. In Portugal and Spain, add twenty percent, because rents in the student cities have risen faster than published cost-of-living data reflects.

Research the specific city rather than the country. Living costs in Warsaw and in Lublin differ by nearly half, and national averages describe neither.

What Tuition Costs Across Budget Europe

Low living costs matter less if tuition consumes the saving, so the two need to be considered together.

Poland: public universities charge non-EU students roughly €2,000 to €4,500 a year for most programmes, with medicine considerably higher. Programmes taught in Polish are free to those who pass the language requirement, which is a genuine route for students willing to spend a preparatory year.

Czech Republic: study in Czech is free at public universities, including for international students. English-taught programmes run roughly €3,000 to €6,000 a year. The free Czech-taught route is the most underused opportunity in Europe, requiring a year of language study for a full degree at no tuition cost.

Hungary: around €3,000 to €6,000 a year for English-taught programmes, a great deal offset for many students by the Stipendium Hungaricum scholarship, which covers tuition entirely and adds a stipend.

Romania and Bulgaria: among the lowest in the EU at roughly €2,000 to €4,000 a year, with medicine programmes attracting international students at €5,000 to €8,000, still far below Western equivalents.

Portugal: public university fees for non-EU students commonly run €3,000 to €7,000, with considerable variation by institution.

Spain: public universities charge per credit, producing roughly €1,500 to €4,000 a year for most bachelors programmes, among the better value in Western Europe.

Estonia: €2,000 to €6,000 a year, with a reasonable number of tuition waivers awarded on academic merit.

Housing: Dormitories Against the Private Market

Housing is the largest line in every budget and the one with the widest range of outcomes.

University dormitories across Central and Eastern Europe are dramatically cheaper than private rental, frequently €80 to €200 monthly including utilities in Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria. They are also basic: shared rooms are common, bathrooms are often communal, and allocation frequently favours domestic students. Apply immediately on receiving an offer.

Private rental in the same cities runs €250 to €500 for a room in a shared flat and €400 to €800 for a studio, with capitals at the upper end. Portugal and Spain have become the expensive exception: rooms in Lisbon and Porto now commonly exceed €400, and Barcelona and Madrid have moved beyond most student budgets entirely, which is why Valencia, Salamanca, Coimbra, and Braga appear repeatedly in sensible plans.

Three practices reduce risk. Arrange temporary accommodation for the first two to four weeks and find permanent housing in person, because remote searching is where nearly all scams succeed. Expect a deposit of one to two months and budget for it separately. And read what the rent includes, since utilities in older Central European buildings can add €50 to €120 in winter and are frequently quoted separately.

Never pay a deposit before seeing a property, in person or on a live video call. The rental scam aimed at incoming international students is the single most common way students lose money in their first month.

Food, Transport, and the Discounts Worth Claiming

Day-to-day costs are where the difference between countries is most visible, and where student status is worth the most.

University canteens are the largest single saving available. A full meal costs €2 to €4 across most of Central Europe, and students who eat lunch there five days a week spend less than those who do not. Cooking at home brings a monthly grocery bill to roughly €120 to €180 in Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria, and €180 to €250 in Portugal and Spain.

Local markets undercut supermarkets considerably for produce in most of these countries, and shopping in the end brings further reductions.

Public transport is heavily discounted for students nearly everywhere. Monthly passes commonly cost €10 to €25 in Central and Eastern Europe, and several German-speaking and Czech cities include transport in the semester fee. In smaller university towns, a second-hand bicycle at €50 to €100 removes the cost entirely.

The ISIC card is worth its modest fee for museum entry, intercity coach travel, and some airline discounts. National student cards frequently provide more, including rail discounts of 50 percent or more in Poland, Czechia, and Romania.

Budget airlines and coach networks make travel between these countries cheap, but book weeks ahead. Last-minute fares eliminate the advantage entirely.

Health Insurance Across the EU

Health cover is compulsory for a residence permit everywhere in the EU, and the cost depends heavily on where you come from.

EU and EEA nationals use the European Health Insurance Card, which covers necessary treatment in the host country at no extra cost. Students from these countries should obtain it before travelling and will rarely need anything else.

Non-EU students generally have two options. Joining the host country's public scheme as a student is usually the cheaper route where available: roughly €15 to €30 monthly in Poland, €25 to €60 in Czechia and Hungary, and €50 to €70 in Portugal. Private student insurance, commonly used where public enrolment is restricted, runs €30 to €90 monthly depending on coverage.

Two points matter for the visa. The policy must meet the minimum coverage level specified by the immigration authority, often €30,000, and a cheaper policy below that threshold results in a refusal. And cover must be in place from the date of entry, not the date of enrolment, so a policy starting in October is insufficient for a September arrival.

Read the exclusions on private policies. Pre-existing conditions, pregnancy, mental health treatment, and dental care are commonly excluded or capped, and students with ongoing medical needs should check this before choosing on price.

Working While You Study, Country by Country

Part-time work rights vary and are worth checking before assuming you can supplement your budget.

Poland allows non-EU students with a valid residence permit to work without a separate work permit, full time, with no hourly cap. This is among the most generous arrangements in the EU.

Czech Republic permits students holding a long-term residence permit for study to work without a work permit.

Hungary allows 24 hours weekly during term and 90 days full time during holidays for those on a student residence permit, with Stipendium Hungaricum holders subject to their own scholarship conditions.

Romania and Bulgaria both permit part-time work, typically capped around 20 to 24 hours weekly.

Portugal and Spain allow student work up to 20 hours weekly, with Spain requiring the employer to apply for authorisation in most cases.

Realistically, typical student wages in Central and Eastern Europe run €4 to €7 an hour, producing €300 to €500 monthly at part-time hours. In Poland and Czechia that covers a dormitory room and food. It does not cover tuition, and visa applications built on anticipated earnings rather than demonstrated funds are refused everywhere.

English tutoring, university research assistance, and remote freelance work generally pay considerably better than local hospitality work, and the first two also strengthen your CV.

Banking, Currency, and Moving Money

Several of these countries are outside the euro, which introduces costs students rarely plan for.

Poland uses the złoty, Czechia the koruna, Hungary the forint, Romania the leu, and Bulgaria has been adopting the euro following its accession timetable. Holding funds in one currency and spending in another means every transaction carries a conversion cost, and card issuers frequently add two to three percent on top of a poor rate.

Open a local bank account in the first month. Most of these countries require an address registration and a residence permit or confirmation of application, which is why the registration sequence matters. Several banks offer free student accounts.

For transfers from home, specialist services consistently beat bank wires, often by several percent on the exchange rate alone. On a year of living costs the difference is worth a month's rent. Multi-currency accounts such as Wise and Revolut are widely used by students in the region for exactly this reason.

One risk worth naming: students from countries with volatile currencies or capital controls should establish early whether they can reliably send money abroad each month, and at what cost. A budget that works at today's rate can fail at next year's, and the students who struggle most are usually those whose home currency moved rather than those who overspent.

The Costs Nobody Includes

A short list of items that appear in almost every student's actual spending and almost no student's plan.

Residence permit fees and renewals, typically €50 to €160 and payable annually in several countries. Document translation and legalisation for enrolment and permit applications, which runs per page and adds up across a full document set. Winter clothing, a genuine several-hundred-euro outlay for students arriving from warm climates into Central European winters. Textbooks and course materials, lower than in the US or UK but not zero, particularly in medicine and engineering. Flights home, which most students take at least annually and which cost most at exactly the times students travel.

Then the irregular ones: a laptop that fails, a dental problem not covered by insurance, a visa appointment in another city, a deposit lost over a disputed inspection.

Holding two months of living costs untouched is the practical protection against all of these. Students who arrive with a precise budget and no margin are the ones who end up in difficulty, not because they planned badly but because they planned for the expected.

Running Several Applications at Once

Most students apply to multiple countries, and the administrative load is heavier than expected because nothing is standardised.

Build one tracking document covering each programme's deadline, required documents, language requirement, tuition, application fee, and portal login. Deadlines across these countries range from January to July for the same autumn intake, and requirements differ enough that memory is not sufficient.

Order several certified copies of every document at once. Transcripts, degree certificates, and their certified translations are each needed per application, and requesting them one at a time means waiting the full processing period repeatedly.

Where a language test is needed, sit it early. Results take two to three weeks to report, scores are valid for two years, and a single test result can serve every application. Sitting it in December for a January deadline is the most common avoidable failure in the whole process.

Apply to a spread rather than a cluster: one or two ambitious choices, two or three where you comfortably meet the stated requirements, and one where admission is close to certain. Students who apply exclusively to competitive programmes in one country with a single deadline have built a plan with one point of failure.
