Teaching English Abroad in 2026: Certification, Salaries, and the Countries Still Hiring

By Kofi MensahPublished September 12, 202615 min read

Teaching English abroad has been sold for two decades as a way to travel cheaply, and that framing has aged badly. The jobs that pay well in 2026 are professional teaching positions with real requirements, and the ones that do not are increasingly not worth taking.

The market has also split. Online teaching, which absorbed a great many first-time teachers before 2022, collapsed as rates fell and the largest platforms restructured. In-person demand, by contrast, has held up and in several countries strengthened, because the shortage is of qualified teachers willing to relocate rather than of people willing to talk on camera.

What You Actually Need

Three things determine which jobs are open to you, and only one of them is negotiable.

A degree. Most countries tie the work visa for teaching to holding a bachelor's degree in any subject. This is immigration law rather than employer preference, which means it cannot be negotiated. Japan, South Korea, China, Vietnam, the UAE and Taiwan all require it. The countries that do not are mainly in Latin America and parts of Europe, where the pay is correspondingly lower.

A certificate. A 120-hour TEFL or TESOL qualification is the baseline and is widely accepted. The CELTA, awarded by Cambridge, is the one that opens better jobs: it runs four weeks full-time, costs roughly $1,600 to $2,500, includes assessed teaching practice with real students, and is recognised everywhere. The distinction that matters is teaching practice. A $40 online TEFL with no observed teaching is accepted by low-tier employers and by nobody else.

For international schools and university positions, a DELTA or a masters in TESOL or applied linguistics is the next tier, and it changes the salary bracket substantially.

A clean background check. Nearly every country now requires a criminal record check from your home country, apostilled. Start this early; it routinely takes six to ten weeks and holds up otherwise complete applications.

Native speaker status is still requested by many employers, though several markets have moved toward accepting any teacher with strong English and a recognised qualification. Non-native speakers with a CELTA and a degree have considerably more options than the job adverts suggest, particularly in Europe, Latin America, and the better international schools.

Where the Money Is

Salary alone is misleading. What matters is what you save after rent, and that reorders the list completely.

The Gulf pays the most by a wide margin. Licensed teachers at international schools in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Qatar earn $3,500 to $6,000 monthly, tax-free, usually with housing, flights and medical cover provided. These positions require a teaching licence and classroom experience, so they are not entry-level. Savings of $2,500 to $4,000 a month are realistic.

South Korea remains the best-structured entry-level market. Public school programmes such as EPIK and private academies pay roughly 2.1 to 2.8 million won monthly, with free furnished housing, a return flight, national health insurance, and a severance payment of one month's salary on completing a year. Savings of $800 to $1,200 monthly are normal for a first-year teacher.

Japan pays 250,000 to 300,000 yen monthly through the JET Programme and private schools. The yen's weakness has reduced the value of that in dollar terms, and housing is usually subsidised rather than free. Savings are modest; people go for the experience rather than the money.

China pays 15,000 to 30,000 yuan monthly at training centres and international schools, with housing or an allowance and flights. Regulation tightened considerably after the 2021 reforms to the private tutoring sector, and the market now concentrates in licensed schools rather than after-school centres.

Vietnam has become one of the best value destinations: $1,500 to $2,200 monthly against low living costs, giving savings comparable to Korea without the same qualification barriers.

Spain, Italy, Czechia and Poland pay €1,000 to €1,800, which covers a modest life and little more. People teach in Europe for the life rather than the savings, and many combine it with private tutoring at €15 to €25 an hour.

Latin America pays least, typically $500 to $1,200, generally enough to live on locally and not to save or to service a student loan.

Government Schemes Worth Applying To

Government-run programmes are better structured, better paid relative to the work, and more reliable than most private employers. They are also the least advertised.

  • JET Programme (Japan). Long-running, competitive, applications open in the autumn for the following summer. Strong support structure and a genuine cultural exchange component.
  • EPIK (South Korea). Public school placement with two intakes a year, housing provided, and clearly defined conditions.
  • NALCAP (Spain). The North American Language and Culture Assistants programme places assistants in Spanish schools with a stipend, a student visa, and 12 to 16 contact hours a week. Applications open in January.
  • TAPIF (France). The Teaching Assistant Program in France, 12 hours weekly with a stipend, applications in the autumn.
  • Fulbright English Teaching Assistantships. Available in dozens of countries, competitive, and considerably better regarded on a CV than a private language school post.
  • Ministry programmes in Chile, Colombia, Georgia and Thailand all run similar schemes with lower barriers to entry.

These fill months ahead and most run a single annual cycle, so the calendar matters more than in the private market.

Reading a Contract Before You Sign

Most bad experiences in this field trace to a contract nobody read properly. Establish the following in writing.

Teaching hours and contact hours. A contract specifying 40 hours a week may mean 25 teaching hours plus office time, or 40 teaching hours, which is a completely different job. Get both figures.

Who holds your visa. Working on a tourist visa is illegal in every country listed here and is still common at the low end of the market. It exposes you to deportation and a re-entry ban, and it removes any protection if the employer stops paying.

Housing specifics. Provided, or an allowance? Furnished? Who pays utilities and the deposit? A "housing provided" clause that turns out to be a shared room 90 minutes from the school is a common complaint.

Flight and severance terms. Many contracts reimburse the flight only on completion of the full year. Know whether it is paid up front or at the end.

The exit clause. What notice must you give, and what does leaving early cost? Some contracts require repayment of flights, visa fees and training, which can run to several thousand dollars.

Overtime and substitution. Is it paid, and at what rate?

The check that takes ten minutesBefore signing, ask to speak with a current foreign teacher at that school, not one the recruiter selects for you. A legitimate employer arranges this without hesitation. One that refuses, or offers only a testimonial, is telling you something. Search the school's name alongside "review" and check the teacher forums, where the same bad employers appear year after year.

Online Teaching in 2026

The honest position is that online English teaching is no longer a viable primary income for most people.

Rates on the large platforms have fallen to $8 to $15 an hour, competition is global, and the scheduling required to reach full-time hours across time zones is punishing. The Chinese regulatory changes removed the highest-paying segment of the market entirely.

Where online work still makes sense is as a supplement or as a specialism. Business English for corporate clients, exam preparation for IELTS and TOEFL, and one-to-one tutoring arranged independently rather than through a platform all sustain rates of $25 to $50 an hour. That requires building your own client base, which takes a year or two, and it is a different business from logging into a platform.

For teachers already abroad, private tutoring alongside a contract is frequently the most profitable hour of the week. Check your visa permits it before advertising.

Is It Worth Doing

It depends entirely on what you want from it, and being honest about that in advance prevents most of the disappointment.

As a year or two abroad in your twenties, it works well. You live somewhere properly rather than visiting, you save money in Korea, Vietnam or the Gulf, and you acquire a skill.

As a career, it works if you treat it as one. That means a CELTA, then a DELTA or a masters, then moving into international schools, universities, teacher training, or curriculum work. Teachers on that track reach $50,000 to $90,000 and build a profession. Teachers who stay on entry-level contracts for a decade find the pay does not move and the work does not develop.

As a way to fund travel indefinitely, it is weaker than it was. Costs have risen faster than salaries in most markets, and the countries that pay well enough to save are the ones with real qualification requirements.

One thing worth planning for from the start: the skills that transfer. Curriculum design, assessment, teacher training, and managing a classroom of thirty are genuinely valuable elsewhere, and teachers who document them concretely move into instructional design, corporate training, and education technology without difficulty. Teachers who describe five years abroad as "taught English" find the transition much harder.

The Hiring Calendar

Recruitment runs on cycles, and applying at the wrong point in the year is the most common reason good candidates end up with poor jobs.

South Korea public schools hire for two intakes, with EPIK applications closing around February for the August start and around August for the February start. Private academies hire year-round, with the heaviest demand in late summer.

Japan runs on the April academic year. JET applications open in the autumn and close in November for the following July departure, which is a long lead time. Private schools hire throughout the year, peaking in January and February.

China and Vietnam hire continuously with peaks in June to August and December to January.

Europe concentrates hiring in August and September for the academic year, with a smaller round in January. Government assistantship schemes close much earlier, generally between November and April for the following autumn.

The Gulf recruits for August starts, with international school hiring running from January through April. This is the earliest and most structured market, and good schools fill by spring.

The practical rule is to begin six to nine months before you intend to start. Jobs advertised three weeks before term are the ones nobody else took.

Documents to Prepare First

The paperwork takes longer than the job search, and starting it late is what delays most departures.

  • Degree certificate, authenticated. Most countries require it apostilled, or legalised through the destination's embassy where the country is not party to the Hague Convention. Six to ten weeks is typical.
  • Criminal background check, authenticated. Same process, same timeline, and it frequently has a validity window of six months, so the sequencing matters.
  • TEFL or CELTA certificate. Some countries require this authenticated too.
  • Passport with at least eighteen months validity. Renew early if you are anywhere close.
  • Medical check. Required by China, Korea, Vietnam and the Gulf, usually on arrival but sometimes before.
  • Reference letters on letterhead with contact details.

Order two or three authenticated copies of the degree and background check at once. You will need one per application in some systems, and repeating the process takes as long the second time.

Teaching Children or Adults

These are different jobs, and people frequently discover which one suits them only after signing a year-long contract.

Young learners means kindergarten and primary, and it dominates the market in East and Southeast Asia. The work is energetic, repetitive, and physically tiring, classroom management matters more than language knowledge, and parents are a significant part of the job. People who enjoy it tend to enjoy it a great deal.

Teenagers in public schools and after-school academies usually means exam preparation, with motivated and unmotivated students in the same room. The teaching is more substantive and the discipline harder.

Adults covers business English, university classes, and general courses for working professionals. Students are there voluntarily, discussions are more interesting, and the hours are worse: early mornings and evenings, because that is when working adults are free. Pay is generally higher and split shifts are common.

Business and corporate English is the best-paid segment for experienced teachers, often delivered on client sites, and it rewards prior professional experience in another field more than any teaching qualification does.

Be honest with yourself before you commit. A contract teaching four-year-olds when you wanted seminar discussion is a long year, and schools rarely reassign.

Money Logistics Abroad

Several practical matters cost teachers real money in the first months.

Arrival funds. Most contracts pay monthly in arrears, which means four to six weeks between arriving and the first salary. Budget $1,500 to $2,500 to cover that gap plus any deposit, even where housing is provided.

Sending money home. Bank wires lose a surprising amount to exchange spreads rather than visible fees. Specialist services are generally cheaper, and the difference over a year is worth a month's rent. Check whether your destination restricts outbound transfers before assuming it is simple.

Tax. You may be liable in the country where you work, in your home country, or both. Several countries have double taxation agreements that prevent the same income being taxed twice, but you usually have to claim the relief rather than receive it automatically. US citizens must file annually regardless of where they live, which catches many people out in their second year.

Pension and severance. Korea's mandatory severance payment of one month's salary per year worked is real money and is forfeited if you leave before completing the contract. Some countries refund pension contributions to departing foreign workers on exit; ask before you leave rather than after.

Student loans. If you are repaying loans at home, check how overseas income is treated. Some systems require you to report foreign earnings and continue repayments, and ignoring this accrues penalties quietly.

Open a local bank account in the first fortnight. Most countries require a residence card or registration number, which is another reason to complete the arrival paperwork immediately rather than in month two.
