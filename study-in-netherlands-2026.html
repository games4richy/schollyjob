How to Study in the Netherlands in 2026: Tuition, Visa Rules, and the Best English-Taught Programs

By Amara DialloPublished July 2, 202618 min read

Ask ten people why they chose the Netherlands over the UK or Germany for a master's degree, and at least seven will mention the same thing first: it is genuinely cheaper without being a downgrade. Dutch universities routinely place in the global top 100, more than 2,100 programs are taught entirely in English, and tuition for non-EU students typically lands somewhere between 8,000 and 20,000 euros a year, well under what comparable UK programs charge and often less than what a public university in the United States would cost even before factoring in the exchange rate. That combination, strong rankings without UK-level price tags, is the actual reason the Netherlands has become one of the fastest growing destinations for international students, now hosting well over 115,000 of them across the country.

This guide covers what studying in the Netherlands actually costs in 2026, which universities are worth prioritizing depending on your field, how the MVV visa and residence permit process works, and the parts most guides gloss over, including the country's genuinely difficult student housing situation. If you are trying to figure out whether the Netherlands is the right fit rather than just another name on a shortlist, this is the version with the real numbers attached.

What makes the Netherlands unusual among European study destinations is how deliberately it has built its entire international education system around English-taught instruction rather than treating English programs as a small side offering layered on top of a primarily Dutch-language system. This was not an accident. Dutch universities made a sustained, decades-long push to attract international talent, and the result is a country where a genuinely competitive share of programs, well over 2,100 at last count, run entirely in English from day one, with no requirement to learn Dutch for admission or graduation, even though picking up the language remains strongly recommended for daily life and, more importantly, for long term career prospects if you plan to stay and work locally afterward.

What Tuition Actually Costs, by Nationality and Level

The Netherlands runs a two tier tuition system, and which tier you fall into makes an enormous difference to your total cost. Students from EU and EEA countries, plus Switzerland and Suriname, pay the statutory fee, a government set rate that sits around 2,530 to 2,694 euros a year depending on the exact academic year, applied uniformly across every public university in the country regardless of program or institution. First year bachelor's students from this group often get an additional discount under a first year halving policy, cutting that already low fee roughly in half for their first year.

EU/EEA statutory fee: approximately 2,530 to 2,694 euros per year | Non-EU bachelor's: 7,500 to 20,000 euros per year | Non-EU master's: 8,000 to 30,000 euros per year, higher for business and engineering | studyinnl.org

Non-EU international students pay institutional fees instead, set individually by each university and program, and these vary far more widely. Bachelor's programs for non-EU students typically run 7,500 to 20,000 euros a year, while master's programs run roughly 8,000 to 30,000 euros a year, with technical, business, and medical programs consistently at the top of that range and arts, humanities, and general social science programs at the more affordable end. Always confirm the exact figure directly on your specific program's official page, since institutional fees are set independently by each university and can shift from one academic year to the next.

Universities Worth Knowing, by Field

Dutch university rankings are useful as a starting point, but which specific university is actually right for you depends much more on your field than on overall prestige. Delft University of Technology, commonly called TU Delft, is the country's top ranked technical university and sits among the top 50 engineering institutions globally, with direct research partnerships with companies including ASML, Airbus, and Shell that translate into real internship and employment pipelines for graduates. Wageningen University holds the number one global ranking in agriculture and forestry, making it the clear choice for anyone in food technology, environmental science, or biotechnology, and its smaller campus town setting also means student housing is meaningfully easier to secure than in Amsterdam or Utrecht.

Leiden University, founded in 1575 and one of the oldest universities in Europe, places students within walking distance of the International Court of Justice and Europol through its dedicated campus in The Hague, a genuine advantage for anyone studying international law, human rights, or global governance. The University of Amsterdam and Erasmus University Rotterdam both carry strong general reputations across business, economics, and social sciences, with Rotterdam in particular recognized for its business school. Utrecht and Maastricht round out the group of universities that consistently appear in shortlists for their combination of teaching quality and reasonable cost of living relative to Amsterdam.

The MVV Visa and Residence Permit Process

Non-EU and non-EEA citizens planning to study for longer than 90 days need both an MVV, the long stay entry visa, and a residence permit for the purpose of study, officially called a VVR. The good news is that you personally do not file this application directly with immigration. Your host university acts as your sponsor and submits the application to the IND, the Dutch Immigration and Naturalisation Service, on your behalf once you have accepted an offer of admission.

What you actually need to provide your university Once admitted, you will typically need to supply your university with your admission confirmation, a valid passport, proof of financial means covering your study period, generally requiring evidence of at least 1,100 euros a month in available funds under current IND rules, and in some cases a tuberculosis test result depending on your country of origin. The visa processing fee itself runs around 207 to 250 euros depending on the exact procedure, and total processing time from submission to decision typically falls between four and ten weeks, so applying as early as your university allows is worth doing rather than waiting until close to your intended start date.

EU and EEA citizens skip this process entirely. They do not need a visa or residence permit to study in the Netherlands, and only need to register with their local municipality, known as the gemeente, within five days of arrival. Every student, regardless of nationality, eventually needs a BSN, the Dutch citizen service number, which functions as the master identifier required for opening a bank account, signing a rental contract, registering with a doctor, and working a part time job.

Cost of Living, City by City

Tuition is only part of the total cost, and city choice affects your monthly budget more than almost any other single decision. Amsterdam carries the highest cost of living by a meaningful margin, particularly for housing, and international students there commonly report monthly costs 500 to 800 euros higher than equivalent students in smaller cities like Groningen. Overall estimates for total living costs, including housing, food, transport, and health insurance, generally fall somewhere between 1,000 and 1,600 euros a month depending on the city and personal lifestyle, with several independent guides converging on a rough annual figure of 12,000 to 16,000 euros for total living expenses outside of tuition.

Health insurance is a cost that catches many international students off guard. Students enrolled for more than 12 months are generally required to carry Dutch health insurance or an approved equivalent, running somewhere between 40 and 130 euros a month depending on the type of coverage and whether you are also working part time alongside your studies. Confirm the exact requirement with your specific university's international office before arrival, since the rules differ slightly depending on your visa category and whether you take on part time work.

A worked example makes this more concrete than ranges alone. A master's student in Utrecht, a mid-sized university city cheaper than Amsterdam but still centrally located, might realistically budget around 550 to 700 euros a month for a shared room or small studio, 250 to 300 euros for groceries, 100 euros for a public transport subscription, 90 euros for basic health insurance, and 100 to 150 euros for phone, internet, and miscellaneous costs, landing total monthly living expenses somewhere around 1,100 to 1,350 euros before any spending on travel, entertainment, or non-essential purchases. Multiply that across a ten to twelve month academic year and the total living cost, separate from tuition, lands squarely within the 12,000 to 16,000 euro range cited by most independent sources, confirming that the commonly quoted figures are realistic rather than optimistic marketing numbers.

The Housing Crisis Nobody Puts in the Marketing Materials

This is the part most study abroad guides underplay, and it deserves direct treatment rather than a passing mention. The Netherlands is experiencing a genuinely severe student housing shortage as of 2026, particularly acute in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Utrecht, Delft, and The Hague. Waiting lists for student housing corporations such as SSH and DUWO commonly run six to eighteen months, which is longer than most incoming international students have between accepting an offer and needing to move in.

University managed housing exists but is limited, and priority is generally given to first year international students, which means returning students in later years of a program often face a harder search than newcomers. Practically, this means starting your housing search the moment you accept your offer, not after your visa is approved, applying to every available student housing waiting list simultaneously rather than sequentially, and treating any Dutch rental listing that asks for a deposit before a video call or in-person viewing with real suspicion, since housing scams specifically targeting incoming international students have become common enough that Dutch universities now issue direct warnings about them during orientation.

Scholarships Worth Actually Applying For

More than 800 scholarships targeting international students exist across Dutch universities and government programs, though the honest reality is that most individual awards are modest, typically ranging from 2,500 to 6,000 euros a year rather than covering full tuition and living costs the way some flagship programs elsewhere do. The Holland Scholarship, funded jointly by the Dutch government and participating universities, is one of the more accessible options open to students from outside the EEA, typically awarding a one-time amount toward the first year of study rather than funding the entire program duration. The Orange Tulip Scholarship, run through Nuffic in partnership with specific countries including India, Indonesia, and Vietnam, offers a mix of tuition waivers and partial funding tied to bilateral agreements between the Netherlands and those specific countries, so eligibility depends heavily on your nationality.

Individual universities also run their own named scholarships, often tied to specific faculties or countries of origin, and it is worth checking your target university's own funding page directly rather than relying solely on general scholarship databases, since university-specific awards are sometimes underpublicized outside that institution's own website. Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Leiden all maintain dedicated merit scholarship tracks for non-EEA students that are worth checking even if you do not see them listed on broader scholarship aggregator sites, since Dutch universities frequently update and rename these programs from one academic year to the next.

The Orientation Year Visa After Graduation

One of the most underrated advantages of studying in the Netherlands is what happens after you graduate. Eligible graduates can apply for the Orientation Year visa, known locally as the Zoekjaar, a 12 month permit that allows you to stay in the country and search for employment without needing a job offer at the point of application. This is a meaningfully longer and more flexible post-study work window than several competing destinations offer, and it gives graduates real time to build local professional connections and secure a longer term work visa rather than facing an immediate deadline to leave the country the moment their studies end.

Your Dutch student residence permit also grants Schengen zone access across 27 European countries, which matters more than it might initially seem for anyone planning to attend conferences, take internships, or simply build a European professional network during their studies, without needing to apply for a separate visa every time you cross a border within the zone.

How the Application Process Actually Works

Almost all applications to Dutch universities run through Studielink, the national application portal used across the entire higher education system rather than each university running its own separate system. You create a Studielink account, select your intended program, and the system routes your application to that university's own admissions office for the actual academic review, including checking your prior qualifications, language proficiency, and any program-specific requirements like a portfolio, motivation letter, or entrance exam. Because Studielink is centralized, applying to multiple Dutch universities is more streamlined than in countries where every institution runs a completely separate portal, though you still need to satisfy each program's individual admission requirements.

Language requirements for English-taught programs typically call for an IELTS score of 6.0 or higher, or an equivalent TOEFL score, though exact thresholds vary by program and some highly selective courses set the bar higher. If your prior education was conducted entirely in English, some universities waive the formal English test requirement, but this exemption is decided case by case and should never be assumed without direct confirmation from your target program's admissions office.

Numerus Fixus: The Capacity Limits That Catch Applicants Off Guard

A detail that trips up a surprising number of otherwise well-prepared applicants is numerus fixus, a capacity limit applied to specific high-demand programs, most commonly medicine, dentistry, physiotherapy, and a handful of other competitive fields. Unlike most Dutch programs, which admit students on a rolling or standard admissions basis once minimum requirements are met, numerus fixus programs cap total enrollment and use a separate, often more competitive selection process, sometimes involving additional testing or a weighted lottery system, even for applicants who fully meet the standard academic requirements.

If your target field falls into this category, the application deadline is often significantly earlier than for standard programs, sometimes as early as January for a September start, rather than the more typical spring deadlines that apply elsewhere in the Dutch system. Missing this earlier window is one of the more common and entirely avoidable reasons strong candidates end up unable to start in their intended year, so confirming whether your specific program is numerus fixus should be one of the first things you check, not something discovered halfway through preparing your application.

Working Part Time While You Study

International students on a Dutch residence permit for study purposes are generally permitted to work part time alongside their studies, which meaningfully helps offset the living costs detailed earlier. Non-EU students typically face a cap on part time work hours during the academic year, commonly around 16 hours a week, or full time during the summer months, though the exact rules depend on your specific residence permit category and should be confirmed with your university's international office before accepting any work. EU and EEA students face no such restriction and can work under the same conditions as Dutch citizens.

Part time student jobs in Dutch cities tend to cluster around hospitality, retail, and campus roles such as library or student union positions, and while the pay is reasonable relative to living costs, it should be treated as a supplement to your existing budget rather than a primary funding source, particularly given how competitive part time positions can be in cities with large international student populations chasing the same limited pool of jobs.

Understanding the WO and HBO Binary System

The Dutch higher education system is split into two distinct tracks that international applicants often confuse, and understanding the difference matters for choosing the right program. WO, short for wetenschappelijk onderwijs, refers to research universities, the academically oriented track that includes institutions like the University of Amsterdam, Utrecht University, and TU Delft, and leads toward more theoretical, research-driven degrees. HBO, short for hoger beroepsonderwijs, refers to universities of applied sciences, which take a more practical, career-oriented approach with a stronger emphasis on internships and hands-on skill development over pure academic research.

Neither track is objectively better. The right choice depends entirely on your career goals. If you are aiming toward a research career, a PhD, or a field where deep theoretical grounding matters, WO is generally the stronger fit. If you want a degree with a more direct, practical pathway into a specific profession, with substantial built-in work placement experience, an HBO program at an institution like Hogeschool van Amsterdam or The Hague University of Applied Sciences may actually serve you better than a more prestigious sounding WO program that is not actually structured around the career outcome you want. This distinction rarely gets explained clearly to international applicants, many of whom default to WO institutions simply because they recognize the names from global rankings, without realizing an HBO program might align more closely with what they actually want out of their degree.

Is It Actually Worth It Compared to the Alternatives

The honest comparison against the UK, Germany, and English-speaking destinations further afield comes down to a fairly simple trade-off. Germany offers free or near-free public tuition but with far fewer English-taught programs and a language barrier that shapes daily life more than the Netherlands does, where over 90 percent of the population speaks fluent English and most bachelor's and master's programs run entirely in English with no Dutch language requirement for admission. The UK offers a comparable range of English-taught programs but at substantially higher tuition, often 20,000 to 40,000 pounds a year for international students, well above even the higher end of Dutch institutional fees.

Where the Netherlands genuinely underdelivers relative to its reputation is housing availability, which is a real and current problem rather than a minor inconvenience, and it is worth going in with realistic expectations on that front rather than discovering it after you have already committed. That said, the same is increasingly true across most popular European study destinations, and the Netherlands is not uniquely bad on this point so much as more openly documented, since Dutch student unions and university international offices have been unusually direct about warning incoming students rather than glossing over the issue in admissions marketing. For students prioritizing academic quality, a lower overall cost than the UK, and a strong post-study work pathway, and who are willing to start their housing search early and aggressively rather than waiting until visa approval, the Netherlands remains one of the strongest value options in international education as of 2026, and the gap between its reputation and its actual affordability is one of the more genuinely underrated stories in study abroad decision making right now.
