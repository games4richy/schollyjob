10 Fully Funded Masters Programs in the USA With Monthly Stipend for 2026

By Amara DialloPublished June 7, 2026⏳ 13 min read

The standard narrative about graduate study in the US (that it is prohibitively expensive for international students) is accurate for professionally-oriented programs like MBAs, MPAs, and many law degrees. It is not accurate for research-focused Masters and PhD programs in STEM, social sciences, and humanities at research universities. That distinction matters enormously, and most advice for international students does not make it clearly enough.

Fully funded research-track graduate programs at US universities are funded through a combination of tuition waivers and assistantships that cover living costs. These are not rare exceptions. They are the standard funding model for competitive research programs across dozens of universities. The ten programs covered in this article are fully funded in the genuine sense: tuition covered, monthly stipend included, health insurance provided. Here is what each one offers and how to build a competitive application for it.

How US Graduate Funding Is Structured

When a US PhD or research Master's program says it's "fully funded," what they mean is: the university waives your tuition, and you receive either a Teaching Assistantship (TA) or Research Assistantship (RA) that pays a monthly stipend plus health insurance. TA funding comes from the graduate school budget and requires you to assist in teaching undergraduate courses. RA funding comes from a faculty member's research grant and requires you to work on their research project. The monthly stipend for TAs and RAs typically ranges from $1,600 to $3,500/month depending on university, program, and location. In most US cities, this is livable at a basic student standard.

The Programs Worth Knowing About

1. MIT Department of Economics - PhD with full funding

MIT's Economics PhD program funds all admitted students for five years with tuition waiver, annual stipend (approximately $42,000/year), and health insurance. MIT Economics is one of the top three programs in the world. The acceptance rate is under 2%. The application is straightforward in structure but extraordinarily competitive. Research fit with faculty is the primary admission criterion. MIT Economics PhD.

2. University of Michigan - Rackham Graduate School

Michigan's Rackham School funds most PhD programs with full tuition fellowships and stipends of $25,000–$35,000/year. The Rackham Merit Fellowship provides an additional $20,000 for exceptional applicants. Michigan is particularly strong in social sciences, engineering, public policy, and natural sciences. Rackham admissions.

3. Stanford University - Knight-Hennessy Scholars

Knight-Hennessy funds graduate study at any Stanford program - including professional programs like MBA, law, and medicine. Full tuition, $35,000–$40,000/year stipend, and access to the KH leadership program. Deadline: October 6, 2026. Acceptance rate: approximately 2%. See our full Knight-Hennessy Scholars 2027 guide. knight-hennessy.stanford.edu.

4. Fulbright Foreign Student Program

The Fulbright Foreign Student Program brings international students to US universities for Masters or research degrees. The package covers tuition, living costs, health insurance, and travel. Awards are country-specific, administered through Fulbright commissions or US embassies in your country. Deadlines vary by country - most fall between February and October. This is often the most accessible fully funded path to US graduate study for applicants from eligible countries. Fulbright Foreign Student Program.

5-10: Additional Funded Programs

These consistently offer full funding with monthly stipends for international students in 2026: Harvard Kennedy School's Mid-Career MPA (with multiple fellowship tracks providing full tuition plus living support); University of Chicago's fully funded PhD programs across departments; Columbia University's Earth Institute PhD fellowships ($32,000–$38,000/year); Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health full PhD funding; Yale Poorvu Center fellowships for graduate teaching; University of California Berkeley's Graduate Division Fellowships. For all of these, the key is applying in October through December for September entry and specifically requesting funding consideration in your application. At most US research universities, admitted PhD students are automatically considered for assistantship funding - but you must ask for consideration explicitly in your personal statement or funding application.

Why Masters and PhD Funding Work Differently

Professional Masters programs in the US - MBAs, MPPs, most two-year Masters - are almost never fully funded. Research-track Masters programs, especially those that feed into PhD programs, are routinely funded. The distinction is usually visible in program structure: if the program is 30 credits and research-thesis-based, it's likely fundable. If it's 60 credits and coursework-based, it's likely tuition-paying. Check whether the program you're targeting has a thesis track, and ask explicitly during your application whether TA/RA funding is available for Masters students. Many US universities fund Masters students but don't advertise it prominently because the majority of applicants don't ask.

How Assistantships Work

Most funded graduate study in the United States is paid for through an assistantship rather than a scholarship, and the distinction changes what you should be applying for.

A teaching assistantship pays you to teach or support undergraduate courses, typically twenty hours a week. In exchange you receive a tuition waiver and a stipend, commonly $18,000 to $35,000 over nine months depending on the institution and the region. A research assistantship works identically but pays you from a faculty member's grant to work on their research, which makes it dependent on that grant existing.

The critical point for masters applicants is that assistantships are abundant at doctoral level and scarce at masters level. Departments fund PhD students because they need them for five years; masters students are frequently expected to pay. This single asymmetry explains why so many applicants find funding easier to obtain for a longer degree than a shorter one.

Where masters assistantships do exist, they concentrate in a few places: large public universities with heavy undergraduate teaching loads, laboratory sciences and engineering where research grants fund bodies, and departments where the masters is a recognised route into the PhD.

Tuition waivers vary in generosity. A full waiver covers all tuition; a partial waiver covers a portion; and some institutions waive tuition but still charge serious mandatory fees of $1,500 to $4,000 a year. Read what is covered rather than assuming a funded offer means no costs.

Aid for International Students, and Why FAFSA Does Not Apply

US financial aid discussion is dominated by federal aid, none of which is available to international students, and the resulting confusion wastes a great deal of applicant effort.

The FAFSA governs federal grants and loans, and eligibility requires US citizenship or permanent residence. International applicants should not complete it. What some institutions do require instead is the CSS Profile or their own international financial aid form, used to assess need-based institutional aid from the university's own funds.

A small number of universities, mostly well-endowed private institutions, meet full demonstrated need for international students. At that level the aid is heavy. At most other institutions, need-based aid for international students is limited or absent, and merit awards are the realistic route.

Two practical consequences. First, the sticker price of a private university with strong aid can be lower in practice than a public university with none, and applicants who filter on published tuition eliminate their best options. Second, a small number of institutions are need-aware for international applicants, meaning applying for aid affects your admission chances. Where that is stated, it is worth knowing before deciding how much aid to request.

External funding (Fulbright, your own government's scholarship scheme, employer sponsorship) resolves this entirely and is treated favourably by admissions offices, since a funded applicant costs the department nothing.

Applying: Deadlines, Fee Waivers, and Whether You Still Need the GRE

The application cycle runs earlier than most international applicants expect, and funding deadlines run earlier still.

For an autumn start, most funded programmes close between early December and mid-January. Programmes with later deadlines frequently note that applications received after a certain date will not be considered for funding, which is a different thing from not being considered for admission. Submitting in February to a programme that accepts applications until April usually means paying your own way.

The GRE has moved considerably. A large number of programmes dropped the requirement after 2020 and many have not reinstated it, particularly in the humanities, social sciences, and public policy. It remains common in economics, engineering, and some sciences. Where a programme states the GRE is optional, submitting a strong score still helps and submitting a weak one does not, so take it only if you can perform well.

Application fees of $75 to $125 per programme accumulate quickly across eight applications. Fee waivers exist more widely than applicants realise, granted for demonstrated financial need, participation in certain programmes, or on request to the graduate school. Ask. Departments also waive fees for applicants they have met at a conference or recruitment event.

Apply to a spread of eight to twelve programmes across a range of selectivity. Funded admission is competitive enough that a list of five highly selective departments is a plan with a high probability of producing nothing.

Contacting Faculty Before You Apply

In research-oriented programmes, particularly in the sciences, contacting potential supervisors before applying materially affects outcomes. In professional masters programmes it matters much less.

Where it matters, the email that works is short and specific. Name the paper of theirs you read and the question it raised. State in two sentences what you would want to work on. Mention your background in one line. Ask whether they expect to take students in the coming cycle. Under two hundred words.

What fails: long messages, attached full CVs unrequested, generic admiration, and identical emails sent to six faculty in the same department, which they notice because they talk to each other.

A faculty member who replies positively can advocate for your application internally, and in departments where admission decisions are effectively made by individual supervisors, that is decisive. A non-reply means nothing in particular; academics receive a great many of these.

Timing matters. September to November is right for a December or January deadline. Writing in February, after decisions are underway, is too late.

Reading a Funding Offer Properly

Offer letters are frequently ambiguous, and accepting one without understanding it causes real difficulty in year two.

Establish the duration. A guarantee of funding for one year in a two-year programme means you will be competing for support again next spring. Multi-year guarantees exist and should be confirmed in writing.

Establish what the waiver covers. Tuition only, or tuition plus mandatory fees? Health insurance is a significant cost, often $2,000 to $4,000 annually, and whether it is covered varies by institution.

Establish the stipend period. A figure quoted as an academic-year stipend covers nine months, leaving three months unfunded, and summer funding is usually separate and not guaranteed.

Establish the work requirement. Twenty hours a week of teaching in a demanding programme is a real constraint, and some assistantships expect more in practice than on paper.

Establish the conditions for renewal, which typically include a minimum grade point average and satisfactory progress, and ask what proportion of students have lost funding under those terms.

It is entirely acceptable to email the graduate coordinator with these questions before accepting, and the quality of the answer tells you something about the department.

Getting the Most From Your Recommenders

Letters of recommendation carry more weight in US graduate admissions than in most systems, and applicants consistently under-manage them.

Choose people who supervised your work directly over people with impressive titles who barely know you. A committee reading a detailed letter describing how you handled a specific problem learns something; a letter confirming you took a course and performed well says nothing they cannot read from your transcript.

Ask at least six weeks ahead, and ask whether they can write a strong letter rather than whether they will write one. The phrasing gives a reluctant referee a graceful exit, which is better for you than a tepid letter.

Send each referee a package: your statement of purpose draft, your CV, the list of programmes with deadlines and submission links, and a short note reminding them of specific work you did together, with dates and outcomes. Academics write many of these and remember less than you assume.

Waive your right to view the letters where the form offers the choice. Committees treat non-waived letters as less candid, and the waiver is standard practice.

Follow up a week before each deadline, politely. Referees miss deadlines routinely and are generally grateful for the reminder rather than irritated by it. An application incomplete because of a missing letter is not reviewed.

The F-1 Visa and Working During Your Degree

Work rights on a student visa are narrow, specific, and strictly enforced.

On an F-1 visa you may work on campus up to twenty hours a week during term and full time during breaks, without additional authorisation. This covers assistantships, library and administrative work, and campus jobs.

Off-campus employment requires authorisation. Curricular Practical Training permits work that is an integral part of your programme, typically an internship connected to your course, authorised by your designated school official. Using twelve months or more of full-time CPT eliminates your eligibility for the next category, which is a trap worth knowing about in advance.

Optional Practical Training provides twelve months of work authorisation in your field, usually taken after graduation, with a twenty-four month extension available for graduates of designated STEM programmes. The STEM extension is one of the strongest practical arguments for choosing an eligible programme, since it provides three years of work authorisation rather than one.

Unauthorised work, including freelance and remote work for employers outside the United States, is a status violation with serious consequences. Students with existing online income should take advice before continuing it.

Maintaining status requires full-time enrolment, so reducing your course load without prior authorisation is a violation even when academically sensible.

The Costs That Are Not Tuition

Funded students still face substantial expenses, and offers are frequently accepted without accounting for them.

Mandatory fees of $1,500 to $4,000 a year are charged separately from tuition at many institutions and are often not covered by a waiver. Health insurance is commonly compulsory at $2,000 to $4,000 annually unless the assistantship includes it.

Then the start-up costs, which arrive before the first stipend payment: a flight, a rental deposit usually equal to one or two months, furniture for an unfurnished apartment, and living expenses for six to eight weeks, since stipends are typically paid monthly in arrears beginning after the term starts. Two to three thousand dollars before any money arrives is a realistic figure.

The SEVIS fee and visa application fee are payable before travel. Textbooks in some fields remain expensive. And the nine-month stipend structure means three summer months with no income unless you secure summer funding, which is a recurring cause of financial stress among graduate students.

A stipend of $24,000 in a university town in the Midwest supports a modest but adequate life. The same figure in Boston, New York, or the Bay Area does not, and comparing offers without adjusting for local rent produces the wrong decision.

After Graduation: H-1B and the Alternatives

Post-study employment in the United States is more constrained than in most comparable destinations, and planning for it should begin well before graduation.

The standard route is Optional Practical Training followed by an employer-sponsored H-1B visa. The difficulty is that H-1B is subject to an annual cap, allocated by lottery, and demand routinely exceeds supply by a wide margin. Selection is random among registered applicants, which means a strong candidate with a willing employer can still be unsuccessful.

Two important exceptions exist. Universities, affiliated non-profit research organisations, and some government research bodies are cap-exempt, meaning they can sponsor H-1B petitions at any time without entering the lottery. Graduates who take positions at these institutions bypass the constraint entirely, and this is the single most underused piece of information in US immigration planning for graduates.

The STEM OPT extension provides three years rather than one, which allows three lottery attempts rather than one and dramatically improves the cumulative odds.

Other routes include the O-1 for those with a demonstrable record of achievement, the J-1 with its two-year home residency requirement where applicable, and employer transfer to an overseas office followed by an L-1 return.

Applicants for whom remaining afterwards is the primary objective should weigh this against destinations with clearer post-study routes. Our guides to studying in Canada and studying in Germany cover systems where the path from graduation to residence is considerably more predictable.
