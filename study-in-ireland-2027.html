Study in Ireland 2027: Tuition, the Student Visa, and the Two-Year Graduate Stay Back

By Amara DialloPublished October 22, 202615 min read

Ireland has quietly become one of the strongest destinations in Europe for international students, and the reason is what happens after graduation rather than during the degree. A master's graduate can stay for two years on a graduate permission with no job offer and no sponsor, work in any field, and then move onto an employment permit that leads to long-term residence.

It is also English-speaking, inside the EU, and home to the European headquarters of a large share of the world's technology and pharmaceutical companies. What it is not is cheap. Tuition for non-EU students runs to the level of a mid-range UK degree, and Dublin's housing market is the single hardest part of the move.

What Tuition Costs

Ireland operates a two-tier fee system. EU and EEA students pay a low or free rate at undergraduate level under the Free Fees Initiative, plus a student contribution charge. Everyone else pays the non-EU rate, and that is the figure that matters here.

For non-EU undergraduates, fees commonly run from about €9,850 to €25,500 a year, depending on the university and the subject. Business, humanities and social science programmes sit at the lower end. Engineering, computing and laboratory sciences sit higher. Medicine and health sciences are in a different bracket, frequently €39,500 to €55,000 a year, which makes Irish medical degrees among the most expensive in Europe.

Taught master's programmes for non-EU students generally fall between €10,000 and €28,000 for the year, with business and computing at the top and arts and social sciences lower. Research degrees are usually cheaper, and funded PhD positions frequently cover fees entirely and add a stipend.

Check the exact figure on the university's own fees page for your specific programme and your year of entry. Irish universities publish these clearly, and third-party summaries go stale quickly.

The Universities Worth Knowing

Ireland has a small higher education system, which makes the choice simpler than in larger countries.

Trinity College Dublin is the oldest and the most internationally recognised, strong across humanities, law, business and the sciences. University College Dublin is the largest, with particular depth in business, agriculture, veterinary medicine and engineering. University College Cork and the University of Galway both have strong medical, marine and life sciences faculties and considerably lower living costs than Dublin.

Dublin City University is strong in computing, communications and business, with a well-organised work placement culture. The University of Limerick pioneered cooperative education in Ireland and remains one of the best choices for anyone who wants a paid placement built into the degree. Maynooth University and the technological universities, including TU Dublin, offer lower fees and strong applied programmes.

The practical point for most applicants is that studying outside Dublin cuts living costs substantially while leaving the graduate permission and employment routes identical.

How to Apply to Irish Universities

The route depends on the level.

Undergraduate applications from most international students go through the Central Applications Office, though many universities accept direct applications from non-EU students. Check which your target institution expects, because it differs.

Postgraduate applications go either through the Postgraduate Applications Centre or directly to the university, again depending on the institution. Most taught master's programmes open in the autumn for the following September and assess on a rolling basis, which matters more than the closing date: popular programmes fill before the published deadline, and applying in October gives you a materially better chance than applying in April.

Typical requirements are a recognised bachelor's degree at the stated classification, English proficiency (usually IELTS 6.5 with no band below 6.0, or the equivalent), transcripts, a personal statement, and one or two references. Applicants from countries where instruction is in English can frequently substitute a medium of instruction letter, though the policy varies by university.

The Student Visa and Proof of Funds

Whether you need a visa before travelling depends on your nationality. Visa-required nationals apply online through the AVATS system for a D study visa, then attend a visa office or embassy with supporting documents. Non-visa-required nationals travel first and register on arrival. Everyone from outside the EEA must register their permission with immigration after arriving and receive a residence permit.

The financial requirements are specific and strictly applied:

  • Evidence of at least €10,000 available to you for living costs for the first year, and evidence that you will have the same amount for each subsequent year.
  • Proof that at least €6,000 of your tuition fee has already been paid before you apply for the visa, or the full fee if the course costs less than €6,000.
  • Private medical insurance covering at least €25,000 for accident and €25,000 for illness.
  • A letter of acceptance for a full-time course on the official Interim List of Eligible Programmes.

Two details catch people out. The €10,000 must be genuinely accessible to you, and funds held by a distant relative are treated with suspicion. And the course must appear on the official eligible programmes list; private colleges offering courses outside that list cannot support a student permission.

The full current requirements are published by Immigration Service Delivery, which is the authority to rely on rather than any agent's summary. Processing times vary by country and commonly run to eight weeks or more, so apply as early as the rules allow.

If you have been refused a study visa elsewhere, declare it. Our guide to reapplying after a student visa refusal explains why concealment converts a survivable refusal into a far more serious problem.

Living Costs, and the Housing Problem

Budget realistically. Outside Dublin, a student can live on roughly €900 to €1,200 a month. In Dublin, €1,300 to €1,800 is closer to the truth, and rent is most of the difference.

Student accommodation in Dublin commonly runs €900 to €1,400 a month for a purpose-built room. Shared private rental is cheaper but scarce. Cork, Galway, Limerick and Maynooth are considerably more affordable, often €500 to €800 for a room in a shared house.

Ireland's student housing shortage is genuine and it is the single most common complaint from international students. Apply for university accommodation the day you receive your offer, and treat the €10,000 proof of funds as a visa requirement rather than a realistic annual budget, because in Dublin it is not one. The Europe study budget guide sets out how to build a monthly budget that survives contact with reality.

One warning worth repeating: never transfer a deposit for accommodation you have not seen, in person or on a live video call. Rental scams targeting incoming international students are common in Dublin and Galway in particular.

Work Rights on a Stamp 2 Permission

Students holding a Stamp 2 permission on an eligible full-time course may work 20 hours a week during term and 40 hours a week during holiday periods, which are defined as June to September and from mid-December to mid-January.

Minimum wage work covers part of your living costs and will not fund tuition. Treat part-time work as a supplement, and note that visa applications built on expected earnings rather than demonstrated funds are refused.

Students on courses that are not on the eligible programmes list, or on Stamp 2A, have no work rights at all. Confirm which permission your course carries before you rely on the income.

The Graduate Permission: Ireland's Strongest Argument

This is the part that makes Ireland worth the tuition for many applicants.

Under the Third Level Graduate Programme, graduates of eligible Irish awards can remain in Ireland on a Stamp 1G permission to look for work or take up employment, with no sponsor required and no restriction on the field.

  • Level 8 graduates, meaning an honours bachelor's degree, may stay for up to 12 months.
  • Level 9 and 10 graduates, meaning master's and doctoral awards, may stay for up to 24 months, granted as an initial 12 months with a further 12-month renewal.

The renewal for the second year is not automatic. You must show genuine job-seeking activity, such as submitted applications, interview correspondence, or recruitment agency registration, so keep records from the start.

There is an overall ceiling on time in Ireland as a student and graduate combined: seven years for Level 8 graduates and eight years for Level 9 and 10 graduates. The official conditions are on the Third Level Graduate Programme page.

The practical consequence is straightforward. A one-year taught master's buys you two years of unrestricted work permission afterwards, which is a better ratio than almost anywhere else in Europe.

From Graduate Permission to Long-Term Residence

The graduate permission is a runway, not a destination. The route onward runs through an employment permit.

The Critical Skills Employment Permit is the one to aim for. It applies to occupations on Ireland's critical skills list, which covers most ICT roles, engineering, many healthcare professions, and a range of finance and science positions. The general salary threshold sits at €38,000 for listed occupations with a relevant degree, and €64,000 for occupations outside the list. Thresholds are reviewed, so confirm the figure at the time of your offer.

Its advantages are substantial: no labour market needs test, immediate family reunification with spousal work rights, and eligibility to apply for Stamp 4 after two years, which removes the tie to a specific employer. Five years of reckonable residence opens the way to long-term residence and, in time, citizenship.

The General Employment Permit covers occupations outside the critical skills list, with a lower salary floor but a labour market needs test and a longer path to Stamp 4.

Scholarships and Funding

Ireland has fewer large scholarship schemes than Germany or the UK, and the ones that exist are competitive.

The Government of Ireland International Education Scholarships provide a stipend plus a fee waiver for one year of study, awarded to a small number of students annually. The Irish Research Council funds postgraduate research. Individual universities run their own non-EU merit scholarships, frequently as partial fee reductions of €2,000 to €5,000, and these are the most realistic funding most applicants will access. Several are awarded automatically on admission, so check whether a separate application is required.

For research students, funded PhD positions attached to a supervisor's grant are the strongest route, covering fees and paying a stipend. Our guide to fully funded PhD programmes explains how to approach a supervisor and what to check in the offer.

Is Ireland the Right Choice?

It suits you if you want an English-taught degree inside the EU, you are targeting technology, pharmaceuticals, finance or medical devices, and the two-year graduate permission matters to your plan. For anyone weighing Ireland against the UK, the graduate stay is comparable while the employment permit route to settlement is more straightforward.

It suits you less well if cost is your binding constraint. Tuition plus Dublin living costs can exceed €40,000 for a single year, and scholarship coverage is thinner than in Germany or the Nordics. Arriving: Registration, Insurance, and the First Month

Several things have to happen in a specific order in your first weeks, and getting the sequence wrong causes weeks of delay.

Immigration registration. Every non-EEA student staying more than 90 days must register with immigration and receive an Irish Residence Permit. In Dublin this is done through the Burgh Quay registration office by online appointment; outside Dublin it is handled by the local Garda station. Appointments in Dublin are released in batches and are frequently the hardest part of arrival, so start checking the day you land. You will need your passport, your college letter, proof of fees paid, proof of private medical insurance, and the registration fee.

Medical insurance. Private cover of at least €25,000 for accident and €25,000 for illness is required for registration, not only for the visa. Many universities offer a group policy that satisfies the requirement and costs less than buying individually. Confirm the policy meets the stated minimums before paying, because cheap travel policies frequently do not.

PPS number. You need a Personal Public Service number to work legally or open some accounts. Apply once you have a registered address and your residence permit.

Bank account. Most banks want your residence permit and proof of address, which is why the registration appointment sits upstream of nearly everything else.

Budget for six to eight weeks of costs on arrival. Deposits, the registration fee, insurance, and living expenses all land before any part-time income begins.

Ireland Against the Obvious Alternatives

Most applicants weighing Ireland are also looking at the UK, the Netherlands, or Germany. The trade-offs are clear enough to state plainly.

Against the UK. Tuition is broadly comparable and Dublin rents rival London's. The graduate permission is similar in length, at two years for a master's, but Ireland's employment permit route afterwards is more straightforward: the Critical Skills permit carries no labour market test, brings family with work rights immediately, and leads to Stamp 4 after two years. Ireland also has no equivalent of the UK's Immigration Health Surcharge, which saves a four-figure sum. The UK has more universities and more scholarship money.

Against the Netherlands. Dutch tuition for non-EU students is usually lower, commonly €8,000 to €20,000, and the orientation year gives twelve months rather than twenty-four. Dutch housing is at least as difficult as Irish housing. English-taught provision is deeper in the Netherlands; the post-study window is longer in Ireland.

Against Germany. Germany wins decisively on cost, with little or no tuition and an eighteen-month job-seeking permit. Ireland wins on language, since you can build a full career in English, where German employers outside a handful of sectors expect German. If cost is the binding constraint, Germany is the better answer, and our guide to studying in Germany for free sets out the route.

The honest summary: Ireland is an expensive degree with an unusually clean path from graduation to employment to residence, in English, inside the EU. Whether that is worth the premium depends entirely on whether you intend to stay.

The deciding question is what you intend to do after the degree. If the plan is to work in Europe afterwards, Ireland's graduate permission and critical skills route make the arithmetic work in a way that a cheaper degree with no post-study rights does not.
