How to Negotiate Your Salary in 2026: Scripts, Tactics, and What Not to Say

By Kofi MensahPublished June 9, 2026⏳ 13 min read

Most people accept the first salary offer they receive. Not because the number is right, but because the relief of having an offer at all makes asking for more feel reckless. That instinct is understandable and almost always costly. Research consistently shows that candidates who negotiate earn far more over the course of their careers than those who accept initial offers, not because they are more qualified, but because they ask. This guide covers exactly how to ask, what to say, what not to say, and how to handle the specific responses that negotiation conversations produce.

The First Principle: Whoever Names a Number First Loses Ground

Salary negotiation research consistently shows that the first number mentioned in a negotiation anchors the entire conversation. If you name a number first and it's below the employer's budget, you've permanently capped what you'll receive. If it's above their budget, you risk seeming out of touch. The optimal strategy is to delay naming a number for as long as reasonably possible - until you have as much information as possible about the role, the team, and the budget.

When asked "What are your salary expectations?" before an offer has been made, use this deflection: "I want to make sure I have a full picture of the role and responsibilities before settling on a number. Could you share the budgeted range for this position? I'm confident we can find something that works for both of us." This is professional, non-evasive, and buys you valuable information. Most employers will either share the range or confirm that there's flexibility - both of which help you.

After the Offer: The Script That Works

You've received an offer. The number is lower than you wanted. Here's the exact framing that is respectful, direct, and effective in 2026: "Thank you - I'm excited about this role and the team. Based on my research into market rates for this skill set in this market, and given my [specific qualifications/experience], I was expecting something closer to [your number]. Is there flexibility in the base salary?" Then stop talking. Silence after a counter-proposal is not awkward - it's giving the other party time to think. Filling the silence with justifications or apologies weakens your position.

Your target number should be at the top of what you can credibly justify - not the absolute maximum you'd ever accept, but above the midpoint. If the market range is $80,000–$100,000 and you want $90,000, counter at $97,000. This gives both parties room to land at $90,000–$93,000 while feeling like a negotiation occurred.

The Research You Must Do Before Any Negotiation

You need data, not intuition. Use Glassdoor filtered by role, company, and location. Use LinkedIn Salary for industry and company-size breakdowns. Use Levels.fyi for tech roles specifically - it shows total compensation including equity and bonus, which is often as important as base salary. Talk to people in your network who do similar work. The number you bring to a negotiation should be defensible with specific data sources, not just a feeling that you deserve more.

When the Base Salary Is Truly Fixed: Negotiate the Package

Employers often have more flexibility in total compensation than in base salary because different line items come from different budgets. If the hiring manager cannot move on base, consider negotiating: signing bonus (often one-time, easier to approve), extra vacation days, remote work flexibility, professional development budget, earlier performance review, equity or stock options, and title (which affects your market value in future negotiations). The combination of a $5,000 signing bonus and an extra five vacation days may be more valuable to you than a $3,000 base salary increase, depending on your situation. Total compensation is the number that matters.

Phrases That Kill Negotiations

"I need this because of my rent/mortgage/loans." Your personal expenses are irrelevant to a negotiation - employers pay market value for output, not for your cost of living. "I'm sorry to ask, but..." Apologizing before you've even made your request signals that you don't believe you deserve what you're asking for. "My current salary is X." You're under no obligation to disclose this in most jurisdictions. It anchors the conversation to your past and limits your upside. "This is my final offer." Unless it is, ultimatums create adversarial dynamics and sometimes result in offers being withdrawn. Keep the tone collaborative throughout.

Negotiating a Raise at Your Current Employer

The leverage dynamics are different when negotiating with a current employer. You can't use a competing offer as leverage unless you have one, because the logical response is to let you take it. What you can use is: a documented record of your contributions since your last review (quantified wherever possible), market data showing that equivalent roles pay more, and a specific number based on both. The strongest raise request sounds like: "Over the past year, I've [specific achievements with numbers]. Based on market research, the current range for this role at comparable companies is [range]. I'd like to discuss adjusting my compensation to [number] to reflect both my contributions and the current market." Request the conversation in advance rather than springing it at a regular check-in - it signals you're serious and gives the manager time to go to budget discussions prepared.

Finding Salary Data You Can Rely On

Negotiation without numbers is just confidence, and confidence folds the moment someone asks what you are basing it on. The problem is that the easiest sources are the least reliable.

Aggregator averages pulled from self-reported profiles skew low and blur every variable that matters. A "software engineer" average blends a two-year-old in a small city with a staff engineer in Zurich. The figure is technically true and useless to you.

Better sources, roughly in order of usefulness:

  • Mandatory pay ranges in job postings. Several US states, the EU pay transparency directive, and a growing number of employers now publish bands. Search live postings for your title in your market and read the ranges directly. This is the closest thing to ground truth available to an outsider.
  • Public sector and institutional scales. Universities, the UN common system, national health services, and government departments publish grade tables. Even if you are not applying there, they anchor what a level is worth in that country.
  • Recruiters who place your role. A specialist recruiter will usually tell you the band for a role they are filling, because it saves them time. Ask what the range is before you name anything.
  • People doing the job. The direct question is easier than it looks if you ask about the band rather than the person: "Do you know roughly what the range is for this level here?" People answer that far more readily than "what do you earn?"

Collect three to five data points, then work out where you sit inside the range rather than aiming at its midpoint by default. Someone with the exact domain experience the team lacks is not a midpoint candidate.

Negotiating Without a Competing Offer

Most advice assumes you are holding two offers. Most people are not, and the assumption makes the whole subject feel closed to them.

A competing offer is use, but it is not the only draw on, and it is not even the strongest kind. The employer has already spent weeks and considerable money reaching a decision about you. Reopening a search costs them far more than the few thousand separating your number from theirs. That asymmetry exists whether or not anyone else has made you an offer.

What works without one is evidence and specificity. Rather than "I was hoping for more," try naming the gap and the reason: "The range I've seen for this scope in this market sits closer to X. Given that the role owns the reporting function outright rather than supporting it, is there room to move toward that?" You are not claiming another employer wants you. You are pointing at the market and at the job description they wrote.

The second lever is the thing they were worried about. If the interviews kept returning to one gap in the team, and you are the candidate who closes it, that is the sentence to use. Employers pay for the specific problem, not for general excellence.

Never invent a competing offer. It is checkable more often than people expect, it collapses badly, and a rescinded offer over a fabricated one is a bad day.

The Parts of the Package That Are Easier to Move Than Salary

Base pay is frequently the least flexible number in the offer, because it sits inside a band that the hiring manager does not control and that determines everyone else's pay in the team. When the answer on base is a real no, the negotiation is not over.

Signing bonus. Usually the easiest concession available. It comes from a different budget line, does not disturb internal pay parity, and needs fewer approvals. If base cannot move, this is the first thing to ask about. Check the clawback period: many require repayment if you leave inside twelve or twenty-four months.

Start date. Worth real money if you have unpaid notice, want a break, or need to finish a qualification. It is also the cheapest thing for them to grant.

Annual leave. Often fixed by policy, occasionally not. Above a certain level, an extra week is frequently available to whoever asks.

Remote and hybrid terms. Get the arrangement written into the offer letter rather than agreed verbally with a manager who may be gone in a year. This is one of the most valuable and most commonly lost concessions.

Title. Free to the employer and compounding for you, since your next role is benchmarked against it. If the level is borderline, ask.

Training and professional budget. A named figure for courses, certifications, or conferences, written down. Verbal promises about development evaporate when budgets tighten.

An early review. If they cannot move now, ask for a formal salary review at six months with written criteria. Vague promises of "we'll revisit it" are worth nothing; a dated review with defined targets is worth something.

Relocation and visa costs. For international moves, ask who pays for the visa, the flights, the shipping, and the first month of accommodation. These are routinely covered and almost as routinely not offered unless raised.

Equity, Bonuses, and Numbers That Are Not Quite Numbers

Offers increasingly include components that look like compensation but are not cash, and they are where inexperienced candidates lose the most.

Equity at a private company is a lottery ticket with paperwork. Before assigning it any value, ask four questions: how many shares, out of how many total outstanding (a number of shares alone is meaningless), at what strike price, and on what vesting schedule. A standard schedule is four years with a one-year cliff, meaning you receive nothing if you leave inside twelve months. Ask what happens to vested options if you leave: a short exercise window can force you to buy shares you cannot afford or forfeit them.

Equity at a public company is more straightforward, since the shares have a price. Confirm whether the grant is a fixed dollar value converted at grant date or a fixed number of units, and whether refreshers are typical after year one. Many offers front-load the first year and quietly decline afterwards.

Performance bonuses need two answers: what percentage of it has paid out for the last three years, and who decides. A "target 20%" that has paid 8% twice running is a 8% bonus with optimistic branding. Asking the question is normal and the answer is revealing either way.

Pension and employer contributions vary enormously between countries and employers, and a 10% employer contribution is worth more than a comparable rise in base once tax is accounted for. Compare total cost to employer, not headline salary, particularly when weighing offers in different countries.

Remote Roles and Cross-Border Offers

Remote hiring has created a negotiation problem nobody had fifteen years ago: which country's pay applies?

Employers take three broad positions. Some pay a single global rate for the role. Some pay by the employee's location. Some pay by a tiered system that groups countries into bands. These produce wildly different outcomes for the same work, and the policy is rarely published, so ask directly what it is before discussing numbers.

If the company localises pay, the relevant comparison is not what a colleague in San Francisco earns. It is what strong candidates in your market earn for equivalent remote work, which is usually well above the local office rate and well below the headquarters rate. That middle zone is where the negotiation lives.

Clarify the employment mechanism early, because it changes everything downstream. Direct employment through a local entity, engagement through an employer of record, and an independent contractor arrangement carry different tax treatment, benefits, notice periods, and protections. A contractor rate that looks higher than a salaried offer often is not, once unpaid leave, sick pay, employer pension contributions, and your own tax filing costs are counted. Work out the annual net before comparing.

Ask who covers currency risk if you are paid in a currency other than the one you spend. For long-running roles in volatile currencies this is a material question, and some employers will agree to periodic adjustments if asked at the start.

Why Internal Promotions Pay Less, and What to Do

People who stay and get promoted are frequently paid less than people hired from outside into the same job. This is not a rumour; it is a well-documented consequence of how internal raises are calculated.

An external hire is priced against the market. An internal promotion is usually calculated as a percentage uplift on what you were already earning, which means a low starting salary follows you upward for years. Two people can end up in identical roles fifteen or twenty percent apart, and the one who was loyal is the one behind.

Three things help. Ask what the band for the new level is, not what your increase will be: those are different questions and only the first is useful. Make the case before the promotion is announced rather than after, because once it is public the number is much harder to reopen. And treat a promotion with an uplift far below the band as information: if the organisation will not pay market rate for the work, the market will.

The uncomfortable arithmetic is that changing employer remains the fastest route to a sizeable rise for most people in most fields. That does not mean you should leave. It means you should know what you are giving up by staying, and say so plainly when the review comes round.

Walking Away, and the Mistakes That Cost Real Money

Decide your floor before the conversation starts, and write it down. Not a wish, a floor: the number below which this role is worse than your current situation or worse than waiting. Negotiating without one means every concession feels like progress, and you agree to things at four in the afternoon that you regret by nine the next morning.

If the offer lands below your floor and will not move, decline it warmly and leave the door open. "That's below what I can make work, but I'd like to stay in touch if the scope or budget changes." Employers come back more often than people expect, sometimes months later with a different budget.

A short list of expensive mistakes, most of which are avoidable:

  • Naming a number first in the screening call. Whatever you say becomes the ceiling. Redirect to their range once, politely; if pressed twice, give a researched range with a reason attached.
  • Accepting on the phone. Say you are pleased and ask for the written offer and a day or two. Nobody reasonable objects, and nothing good comes from an immediate yes.
  • Negotiating before the offer exists. Leverage begins the moment they decide they want you, and not one minute earlier.
  • Treating the recruiter as the adversary. Once an offer is out, they are usually on your side: their job is to close the hire, not to underpay you.
  • Asking for many things at once. A list of eight requests reads as difficult. Two or three, ranked, with your priority stated, reads as reasonable.
  • Apologising. "I'm so sorry to ask, I know this is awkward" invites the answer no. Asking about money at an offer stage is an ordinary part of hiring, and every manager on the other side has done it themselves.

One closing point. Resign only after the signed contract is in your hand, with the start date, salary, and any agreed terms written into it. Verbal offers are withdrawn more often than anyone admits, and an accepted resignation is very hard to reverse.
