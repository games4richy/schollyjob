Germany Opportunity Card 2026: Points, Proof of Funds, and How to Find a Job After You Arrive

By Kofi MensahPublished September 17, 202615 min read

Most work visas require a job offer before you travel. Germany's Opportunity Card, the Chancenkarte, does not. It lets qualified people from outside the EU move to Germany for up to a year to look for skilled work in person, and it lets them earn money while they search.

That makes it one of the most useful routes into the European job market for anyone who has spent months sending applications from abroad and hearing nothing back. Being physically in the country, able to attend interviews at a day's notice and to start trial work immediately, changes the response rate considerably. It also comes with conditions that are easy to underestimate, above all the money you must show before you are allowed in.

What the Opportunity Card Gives You

  • Up to 12 months of residence in Germany to search for qualified employment.
  • Part-time work of up to 20 hours a week in any job, whether or not it relates to your qualification.
  • Trial employment of up to two weeks per employer, letting a company test you in the role before hiring.
  • An extension of up to two further years where you have secured qualified employment but do not yet meet every condition of a standard work permit.
  • A direct switch from inside Germany to an EU Blue Card or skilled worker residence permit once you land a qualifying job.

What it does not give you is a job, a guarantee of one, or access to social benefits. You fund yourself throughout, which is why the proof-of-funds requirement is so central.

The Two Ways to Qualify

Route one: you are already a recognised skilled worker. If your foreign university degree or vocational qualification is fully recognised as equivalent to a German one, you qualify without the points system. You still need to show you can support yourself.

Route two: the points system. Most applicants use this route. It has three baseline conditions and then a points threshold.

The baseline conditions are:

  • A foreign university degree or a vocational qualification requiring at least two years of training, recognised by the state where it was obtained.
  • Language ability of at least basic German at level A1, or English at level B2.
  • Proof of funds to cover your living costs for the duration of your stay.

Meet those three, and you then need at least six points from the table below.

The Points Table

These are the criteria as set out by the German government's Make it in Germany portal and summarised by Handbook Germany. Points from different criteria add together, but you only score once within each criterion.

4 points: your foreign qualification is partially recognised in Germany, or you hold permission to practise a regulated profession.

3 points: at least five years of professional experience in the last seven years related to your qualification, or German at level B2.

2 points: at least two years of relevant professional experience in the last five years, or German at level B1, or being under 35.

1 point: German at level A2, or English at level C1, or aged between 35 and 40, or at least six months of previous legal residence in Germany in the last five years (tourist stays do not count), or a qualification in an occupation facing a shortage in Germany, or a spouse or partner who also meets the Opportunity Card requirements and applies with you.

Worked examples

A 30-year-old software developer with a computer science degree, four years of experience, and English at C1, no German. Age under 35 scores 2, two or more years of experience scores 2, English C1 scores 1, and IT is a shortage occupation, scoring 1. Total: 6. Eligible.

A 38-year-old civil engineer with eight years of experience and German at B1. Five years of experience scores 3, German B1 scores 2, age 35 to 40 scores 1. Total: 6. Eligible.

A 41-year-old accountant with ten years of experience and English at B2 only. Experience scores 3 and nothing else applies. Total: 3. Not eligible without further language study or partial recognition of the qualification, which alone would add 4.

The last example shows the most effective lever available. Obtaining a partial recognition of your qualification, or reaching German B1, moves most borderline applicants over the line. The government runs an official self-check on the Make it in Germany site that is worth completing before you spend any money.

Proof of Funds: The Requirement That Stops People

You must show you can support yourself without state help. For 2026 the reference figure is €1,091 per month, which works out at €13,092 for a full year.

There are three accepted ways to show it.

A blocked account (Sperrkonto) is the most common. You deposit the full amount with an approved provider before applying, and you can withdraw only up to the monthly limit once you are in Germany. Providers include Expatrio, Fintiba and Coracle, each charging setup and monthly fees, so compare them.

A formal declaration of commitment (Verpflichtungserklärung) from someone resident in Germany, who signs at their local foreigners' authority to cover your costs and must prove sufficient income themselves.

A part-time employment contract in Germany that pays enough to cover the monthly amount, within the 20-hour limit.

The blocked account is the reason many applicants who score enough points still cannot go. Thirteen thousand euros is a substantial sum in most countries, and it must be available before the visa is issued. If it is not realistic for you this year, a sponsored job offer or a funded master's degree may be the better route into Germany. Our guides to jobs in Europe with visa sponsorship and the DAAD EPOS scholarship cover both alternatives.

How to Apply

You apply for a national visa for the Opportunity Card at the German embassy or consulate responsible for where you live. In some countries this runs through an external visa service provider or an online portal.

  1. Complete the self-check on Make it in Germany and confirm you reach six points.
  2. Get your qualification assessed. Check your university and degree in the anabin database. For a partial recognition that earns four points, apply to the relevant recognition authority, which can take several months.
  3. Take a language test from a recognised provider, such as Goethe-Institut or telc for German, or IELTS or TOEFL for English.
  4. Open and fund a blocked account, or arrange the alternative proof.
  5. Book the embassy appointment, which in several countries has the longest wait of the whole process.
  6. Submit your documents: passport, application form, degree and transcripts with translations, recognition documents, language certificates, CV, proof of experience, proof of funds, health insurance for the start of your stay, and a short letter explaining your job-search plan.

The visa fee is set by the embassy and is typically around €75 for a national visa. Processing times vary widely by country, from a few weeks to several months, so start well before you intend to travel.

Health Insurance

You need valid health insurance from the day you arrive. During the job search, most Opportunity Card holders are not eligible for statutory insurance unless they take up employment that makes them compulsorily insured. That usually means private or travel health insurance meeting German requirements for the initial period, switching to statutory insurance once you are employed. Read policy exclusions carefully; cheap travel policies often exclude pre-existing conditions and cap cover at levels the embassy may not accept.

Finding a Job Once You Arrive

Arriving with a year and a clock running is stressful, and planning the search before you leave is what makes the difference.

Target shortage occupations first. IT, engineering, nursing and healthcare, skilled trades, logistics, and education recruit most actively. If your work is in one of these, your odds are strongest. Nurses and other healthcare professionals should read our guide to nursing jobs abroad with visa sponsorship, because professional recognition is a separate and longer process.

Use German job boards, not only international ones. StepStone, Indeed Germany, XING, and the Federal Employment Agency's job portal carry far more roles than English-language international boards. The Federal Employment Agency also offers job-search advice to Opportunity Card holders.

Rewrite your CV for Germany. German employers expect a Lebenslauf with clear month-and-year dates, often a professional photo, and supporting certificates attached. Our guide to writing a CV that gets shortlisted includes the country conventions.

Use the trial employment rule. Offering a company a two-week trial removes much of their hiring risk and is one of the most effective tools the card gives you.

Learn German while you search. Even in English-speaking tech teams, German affects offers, salaries, and daily life. Moving from A1 to B1 during your search year is realistic with serious study and widens the market considerably.

Keep the part-time job within the rulesThe 20-hour limit is strict. Exceeding it, or working as self-employed without permission, puts your residence at risk. Keep your contract and payslips, because the foreigners' authority may ask for them when you switch permits.

Switching to a Work Permit

Once you receive a qualifying job offer, you apply to your local foreigners' authority (Ausländerbehörde) to change your residence title without leaving Germany.

If the salary meets the EU Blue Card threshold, which for 2026 sits at roughly €50,700 for most occupations and about €45,934 for shortage occupations and recent graduates, you can move to a Blue Card, which offers faster permanent residence: after 27 months, or 21 months with German at B1. Otherwise you move to a standard skilled worker residence permit. Check the current thresholds at the time of your offer, as they are adjusted every year.

Appointments at foreigners' authorities in large cities can take weeks or months, so book as soon as you have a signed offer, and keep your Opportunity Card valid while you wait.

Is It Worth It?

The Opportunity Card suits people with a qualification in demand, some savings or a sponsor, and either English at a high level or a willingness to learn German quickly. For those applicants it is among the most flexible routes into a large, well-paying labour market.

It suits people less well if the €13,092 would consume everything they own, if their field is not in demand, or if they speak neither German nor strong English. In those situations a funded degree or an employer-sponsored visa is usually the safer route, even if slower.

What It Costs to Get There

Beyond the blocked account, several smaller costs add up before you arrive. The figures below are typical ranges; your own costs will depend on your country and choices.

  • Blocked account deposit: €13,092 for twelve months, which is your own money and is released to you monthly in Germany.
  • Blocked account provider fees: commonly a setup fee plus a small monthly charge, varying by provider.
  • National visa fee: around €75.
  • Language test: roughly €150 to €300, depending on the test and country.
  • Qualification recognition: from around €100 to €600 for a formal recognition procedure, plus certified copies.
  • Translations and certified copies: charged per page, often several hundred euros for a full set.
  • Health insurance for the job-search period, until employment brings you into statutory insurance.
  • Flight to Germany.
  • First accommodation: a rental deposit, often up to three months' base rent, plus temporary housing while you look.

A realistic total, excluding the blocked account itself, is often €1,500 to €3,500 before your first month's living costs. Housing is the hardest variable. Temporary furnished rooms in large cities are expensive, and many landlords ask for proof of income, which Opportunity Card holders do not yet have. Starting in a smaller city with a strong job market for your field can make the first months far easier.

Bringing a Partner or Family

The Opportunity Card does not generally allow family reunification. Your spouse or children cannot join you as dependants while you hold the card for the job search.

There are two practical workarounds. If your partner also meets the requirements, they can apply for their own Opportunity Card, and applying together earns each of you an extra point. Or your family can join you once you have switched to an EU Blue Card or skilled worker residence permit, both of which allow family reunification and give a spouse the right to work.

Many applicants with families treat the search year as a period apart, which is worth deciding honestly before you commit the savings.

Mistakes That Waste the Year

Arriving without a search plan. Twelve months passes quickly. Before you travel, list target employers, identify the job titles used in German postings for your role, and prepare a German-format CV.

Treating the part-time job as the goal. A 20-hour job pays the rent, but it does not lead to a qualified work permit unless it becomes skilled employment. Guard the time you spend on applications and interviews.

Ignoring recognition. Employers in regulated professions cannot hire you without it, and even in unregulated fields a recognition statement makes your degree easier to understand. Start it before you leave.

Staying in English-only circles. Networking in German, even basic German, opens doors that English-language meetups do not. Local professional associations, trade fairs, and alumni networks are where many jobs are filled.

Waiting too long to switch. Foreigners' authority appointments take time. Book the moment you sign an offer, and do not let the card expire while you wait.

Overstaying or breaking work rules. Both damage future applications, including permanent residence. Keep every document that shows you followed the conditions.
